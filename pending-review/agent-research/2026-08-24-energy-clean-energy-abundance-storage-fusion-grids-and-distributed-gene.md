## Executive Summary

The August 17–24, 2026 evidence does not establish system-wide clean-energy abundance. Storage financing, battery-supply-chain investment, topology optimization, and proposed distributed-resource models provide concrete but limited forward signals. At the same time, temporary emergency extensions for constrained legacy generation, large-load reliability events, transmission and interconnection bottlenecks, uncertain storage duration, and disputes over who pays for data-center growth show that installed or announced capacity is not equivalent to dependable, affordable electricity. Fusion evidence is more cautionary: a company-reported plasma-heating milestone is not net electricity or commercial operation, while peer-reviewed analysis questions the near-term competitiveness of first-of-a-kind fusion plants. For canon review, retain distributed energy and storage as plausible post-singularity infrastructure, but qualify “nearly limitless” abundance as conditional on firm capacity, transmission, interconnection, storage duration, financing, and equitable access. No directional update is warranted for the supplied space, AI-trust, neurotechnology, or social-transition assumptions.

### Fusion economic analysis finds first-of-a-kind fusion plants likely to remain uncompetitive with other low-carbon technologies

- Event date: Published in 2026; exact publication date not established in the surfaced record
- Sources: `S13`, `S14`, `S16`
- Observed fact: A peer-reviewed Nature Energy analysis reported that projected first-of-a-kind fusion-plant capital costs span approximately $1,400 to $43,000 per kilowatt. Using historically grounded experience rates, the authors concluded that fusion power is likely to remain uncompetitive with other low-carbon electricity technologies and questioned whether it would make a timely contribution to decarbonization. The analysis distinguishes physical fusion progress from the economic requirements of a power plant.
- Significance: This is a substantive challenge to treating fusion demonstrations or private financing as evidence of impending clean-energy abundance. Even if a fusion concept achieves plasma gain, commercial deployment still requires affordable construction, maintainability, high availability, component durability, fuel-cycle management, regulation, and competitive electricity costs. The cost uncertainty is large enough that a scientific milestone does not establish an economically useful energy source. The study evaluates projected costs and historical analogues rather than operating commercial fusion plants and does not prove that fusion can never become competitive.
